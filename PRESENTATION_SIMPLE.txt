SLIDE 6: THE CONFOUNDING PROBLEM
---------------------------------
WHY WE CAN'T JUST USE PM2.5 ALONE:

PM2.5 correlates with child mortality, BUT ALSO:
• GDP correlates with both
• Health spending correlates with both
• Urbanization correlates with both
• Fertility rate correlates with both

CONFOUNDERS WE CONTROL FOR:
1. GDP per capita (wealth)
2. Health expenditure (healthcare quality)
3. Urbanization (infrastructure)
4. Fertility rate (family size)

Without controlling = correlation, not causation!

SLIDE 11: CAUSAL INFERENCE
---------------------------
METHOD: Propensity Score Matching (PSM)

GOAL: Estimate causal effect of high vs low pollution

APPROACH:
1. Split: High PM2.5 (>25 μg/m³) vs Low (≤25)
2. Match similar countries on confounders
3. Compare U5MR between matched pairs

RESULT:
• High-pollution countries STILL have higher mortality
• Even after matching on GDP, health, etc.
• Evidence of CAUSAL effect, not just correlation

================================================================
Q&A PREPARATION
================================================================

LIKELY QUESTIONS & ANSWERS:

Q: "Why not use deep learning?"
A: "Great question. Random Forest already achieved R²=0.89, which
    is excellent. Deep learning would add complexity without clear
    benefit. Also, Random Forest is much easier to interpret, which
    is critical for policy applications. We want stakeholders to
    understand why the model makes its predictions."

Q: "How do you know it's causal and not just correlation?"
A: "Two reasons. First, we controlled for major confounders: GDP,
    health spending, urbanization, and fertility. Second, we used
    Propensity Score Matching, which is a quasi-experimental method
    that matches similar countries and compares outcomes. The effect
    persists even after matching, suggesting causality."

Q: "What about other air pollutants like NO₂ or SO₂?"
A: "That's future work. We focused on PM2.5 because it's the most
    harmful to children - the fine particles can penetrate deep into
    lungs. But you're right, a comprehensive model should include
    other pollutants. The challenge is data availability."

Q: "Why is fertility rate so important?"
A: "High fertility means more children per family, which dilutes
    resources - parental attention, nutrition, healthcare access.
    Also, closely spaced births stress maternal health, affecting
    child outcomes. And high fertility correlates with lower
    maternal education, which affects childcare quality."

Q: "Can your model predict for new countries not in the training data?"
A: "Yes, as long as we have the required features: PM2.5, GDP,
    health expenditure, urbanization, and fertility. The model
    learns relationships between these features and mortality, so
    it can generalize to new countries with similar characteristics."

Q: "What's the practical accuracy for policymakers?"
A: "Our Mean Absolute Error is 6.2 deaths per 1,000 live births.
    Given that U5MR ranges from 1 to 130 globally, this is quite
    accurate for policy planning. For most countries, we're within
    3.5 (median error) of the true value."

Q: "Did you try ensemble methods combining multiple models?"
A: "We tested individual models extensively. Random Forest itself
    is an ensemble (of decision trees), which explains its strong
    performance. We could try stacking different models, but given
    Random Forest's 0.89 R², the improvement would be marginal."

Q: "What about data quality issues?"
A: "Good question. Data quality varies by country. WHO PM2.5 data
    uses satellite estimates + ground measurements, which is quite
    reliable. UNICEF U5MR data is from national statistics, better
    for developed countries. We addressed this by using time-based
    validation - if data quality were terrible, the model wouldn't
    generalize to future years, but it does."
